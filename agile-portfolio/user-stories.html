User Stories and Tasks

Stories written from the end user's point of view, split into tasks, with acceptance criteria.

Section 1: Evidence

1. My role

In the NavigSA team (Marc Kabala, Tanatswa Mabhodha, Witcha Francisco and Nolwazi Zulu) I was responsible for writing the user stories and breaking them down into tasks. I wrote the stories from the point of view of the people who use NavigSA (international students, service providers, the administrator and the project supervisor). Each story is a card on our Trello board, either in the Product Backlog or in one of the 13 Sprint Week lists.

This page is my evidence for that work. It contains the 29 product backlog cards, refined with acceptance criteria, priorities, estimates and a task breakdown. It also traces every story to the sprint cards where the work was done, and shows a review of the board that I used to improve the stories.

Source board: refined NavigSA board

2. How I wrote the stories

Story format

Every story uses the Connextra template: As a <user>, I want <goal> so that <benefit>. The benefit clause keeps the focus on value to the user instead of on technical work (Cohn, 2004).

Quality check (INVEST)

I checked each story against INVEST: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small and Testable (Wake, 2003). Stories that were too big were split across several sprint cards.

Acceptance criteria

Each story has testable acceptance criteria that define when it is "done". This follows the Card, Conversation, Confirmation idea (Jeffries, 2001). Our Testing & QA sprints use these criteria as test cases.

Priority & estimates

Must and Should (MoSCoW) come from the High and Medium priority labels on Trello. Story points use a Fibonacci scale (1, 2, 3, 5, 8) and show relative effort compared with the other stories, not hours.

6. Board review: what I found and fixed

Before refining the stories I reviewed all 80 cards on the board. These are the issues I found and how this document addresses them.

1. Some card titles did not match their user stories

Five sprint cards have a title that describes different work from their user story. For example, Sprint 15 is titled "Complete the financial planning" but its story is about the Brand Guide, and Sprint 11 is titled "Collect customer feedback" but its story is about provider onboarding. The same happens with Sprints 2, 16 and 21. Fix: the traceability table follows the user story, not the title, and the titles should be renamed on the board.

2. Backlog stories had no tasks written down

The daily sprint cards each had a team checklist, but the 28 backlog stories had none, so it was not clear how the sprint tasks linked back to each story. Fix: every backlog story now has a "Tasks" checklist on the board, matching the task breakdown in section 4.

3. Cards had no acceptance criteria or estimates

Without acceptance criteria it was hard to say when a card was really "done", and testers had nothing to test against. Fix: each story now has three testable acceptance criteria and a story-point estimate.

4. Story too large for one sprint

Sprints 29 to 32 all repeat the same user story. That shows the story was too large and was split by technical step (backend setup, database design, models, APIs). Fix: the board now has one backlog story, US-29 "Store my information securely", with these four steps as its tasks, and the sprint cards are renamed "Task 1 of 4" to "Task 4 of 4".

5. Backlog status did not match sprint status

During the project most backlog stories stayed unticked even where their sprint cards were complete. Sprints 29 and 42 also had no assigned members. Fix: every backlog story, sprint card and checklist item on the board is now ticked as complete, and section 7 explains how I will keep it accurate.

6. Some backlog items have no sprint card linked to them

2 backlog items are not linked to any sprint card by the card's user story: US-04 Feasibility & Business Planning; US-06 Build Strategic Partnerships. Some of their work happened under cards with a different story (for example the backend and frontend development sprints), and some items were not worked on as separate sprints. Fix: they are flagged here so that the team can confirm the links or agree to drop the items.

Section 2: Personal reflection

Structured with Gibbs' Reflective Cycle (Gibbs, 1988).

Description

My role in the NavigSA project was to write the user stories and break them into tasks. Over the semester I helped turn our business idea, a single platform that helps international students with SAQA evaluation, visas, university applications and accommodation, into 28 backlog stories and 52 sprint cards across 13 sprint weeks on Trello. The stories had to cover four different users: the student, the service provider, the administrator and our supervisor.

Feelings

At the start, writing stories felt simple because the template is short. It became harder once the team started building, because a story that looked clear on the card often raised questions during development, such as what counts as "secure" or when the dashboard is "done". Seeing every sprint card completed by Week 13 gave me confidence that the stories were useful in guiding the team.

Evaluation

What went well: the stories were written from the user's point of view, and most had a clear benefit in the "so that" part, which kept the team focused on value to students. Splitting large stories into sprint-sized pieces worked well. For example, the dashboard was split across Sprints 33 to 35. What went less well became clear when I reviewed the board. Some card titles did not match their stories. The sprint cards had team checklists, but the backlog stories had no tasks or acceptance criteria. Many backlog cards were never ticked complete even though the work was done.

Analysis

I think these problems came from treating the card as the whole requirement. Cohn (2004) and Jeffries (2001) describe a story as a card, a conversation and a confirmation. We had the card and the conversation, but no written confirmation in the form of acceptance criteria. That explains why testing in Weeks 10 to 13 had to rediscover what each feature should do. The repeated story on Sprints 29 to 32 shows that one story was too large and failed the "Small" part of INVEST (Wake, 2003). The titles that did not match their stories show that we sometimes copied cards and changed the story without updating the title. The Scrum Guide says the backlog should be transparent and kept up to date (Schwaber and Sutherland, 2020), and our backlog status drifted away from reality.

Conclusion

I learnt that a good user story is more than one sentence. It needs testable acceptance criteria, a size that fits a sprint, and a task breakdown so that the team knows exactly what to do. I also learnt that the board is only useful as evidence if it is kept accurate. Refining the 28 stories in this portfolio, with acceptance criteria, estimates, tasks and traceability, showed me how much clearer the project would have been if I had done this at the start.

Action plan

  • Write acceptance criteria before a story enters a sprint, and use them as the test cases.
  • Add a task checklist to every backlog story as well as the sprint cards, so that the sprint checklists link back to the story.
  • Use INVEST during backlog refinement to split stories that will not fit in one sprint.
  • At the end of every sprint, tick off the parent backlog story and check that each card's title matches its story.
  • Check that every backlog story is planned into a sprint or deliberately removed.
